# Content Hooks

A hook is a promise the body has to keep. The lever creates the promise. The post pays it. A hook that opens a gap the post never closes buys one click and spends the account's reach to do it.

## The 8 levers

| # | Lever | What it does | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Curiosity gap | Opens a loop the brain wants closed | "99% of people are missing this." |
| 2 | Identity callout | Makes the right person feel seen in 3 words | "If you're [role], read this." |
| 3 | Pattern interrupt | Says the opposite of what they expect | "[X] is dead. Do [Y]." |
| 4 | Exclusivity | Feels like insider access | "This shouldn't be free." |
| 5 | Proof and specifics | Numbers make a claim believable | "How I [result] in [timeframe]." |
| 6 | Loss aversion | Missing out hurts more than gaining feels good | "Read this before you [X]." |
| 7 | Vulnerability | Admitting failure builds instant trust | "I was wrong." |
| 8 | Utility | Signals the post is worth saving | "Bookmark this if [X]." |

Outcome mapping, as the source frames it:

| Stack | Reported signal |
|---|---|
| Curiosity plus utility | Dwell time and bookmarks |
| Pattern interrupt | Replies and quotes, up to 5x |
| Identity plus exclusivity | Shares to friends, the top signal |

Treat those multipliers as directional. They come from one creator's account, not from a controlled test.

## Stack 2 or 3, never 8

Two levers in one line reads as a person. Four reads as a growth course.

```text
I almost quit {thing}. Then I did this.
```

Vulnerability plus curiosity gap. The admission earns attention, the unfinished story earns the click.

```text
{Thing} is dead. Do {replacement}.
```

Pattern interrupt plus utility. It disputes the reader's current practice, then hands them the replacement in the same breath.

Pick the lever first, then write the line. Write the line first and it turns into a pun.

## The body has to pay

Before you commit to a hook, write the payoff paragraph in one sentence. If you cannot, the hook is a lie and the post will read as bait.

- Open a gap, close it in the body. Not in a reply, not in a link.
- The payoff must be as specific as the hook. "99% miss this" needs a named thing they miss.
- A hook about a secret owes the reader the secret.

## Front-load for the fold

Assume one line survives.

- X shows roughly one line before "show more".
- LinkedIn shows 2 lines on mobile, around 200 characters.
- Threads folds early, so the first sentence carries it.

Cut anything before the lever: "I've been thinking about this a lot", "Let me explain", "Here's the thing". Stage directions waste the only line you get.

## Specificity is the multiplier

- "Most people get this wrong" loses to "9 of 10 founders get this wrong".
- Add a number, a timeframe, a role, or a named tool. One of the four is enough.
- False precision invites a fact-check reply. "99.7%" costs you the comment section; "9 of 10" survives.

## Testing a hook

- Write 3 hooks for the same body, one lever each.
- Pick one. Do not A/B on a single account, because reach varies for reasons outside the hook and the comparison teaches nothing.
- Repost the body in 4 to 6 weeks with a different hook. That is the honest test.
- Keep a file of hooks that worked, with the leverage and the payoff. Rewrite them for new posts; never copy them whole.

## Platform notes

| Platform | What the hook has to do |
|---|---|
| X | One line. No hashtags. A reply-bait question in the last line works; in the first line it reads as spam. |
| LinkedIn | 2 visible lines. Utility and proof win. Carousels and docs reward "bookmark this" hooks. |
| Threads | Conversational. Vulnerability and identity callouts carry further than proof. |
| Newsletter subject | Utility plus loss aversion. Subject lines are hooks with a 45-character budget. |

## What kills accounts

- A hook with no payoff, or a payoff that lives in a gated link.
- Four levers in one line.
- Engagement bait ("comment YES") that buys replies and sells nothing.
- Numbers you cannot defend in the replies.
- A viral hook copied verbatim. The audience has seen it, and the algorithm has too.
- Hooks about the poster rather than the reader. An identity callout names them, not you.
