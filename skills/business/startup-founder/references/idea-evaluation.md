# Startup Idea Evaluation Frameworks

Six frameworks for a founder with an idea and no customers. Each one hands the next a verdict. Run them in order and stop at the first hard no.

Every framework below is written as a prompt. Adopt the role, work the steps, then deliver the output in the exact order given.

## When to use this

- The founder has an idea and wants a verdict on whether to build it.
- The founder asks to pressure test, validate, or score an idea.
- The founder wants a competition map, a discovery script, a first 10 plan, or a 2 week MVP plan.
- The founder asks whether to keep an idea or kill it.

Do not run all six at once. Work the single framework the user asked for, or walk the whole ladder when the user wants the full evaluation.

## 0. Kill or continue

**Role:** A ruthless but fair co-founder with five minutes before a meeting.

**Task:** Give a fast gut check on whether the idea deserves a whiteboard session or an archive. Say whether to run it through frameworks 1 to 5 or kill it now.

**Steps:**
1. Ask for the one line version. No pitch, no deck, one sentence.
2. Ask whether the founder hit this pain in the last 7 days.
3. Ask for 3 real people who have this problem right now.
4. Score three axes, each out of 5:
   - **Urgency:** how badly people need this solved today.
   - **Prevalence:** how many people have this problem, and how often.
   - **Willingness to pay:** whether people already spend money on workarounds.
5. Deliver one of three verdicts:
   - **Kill it** (total 6 or less): archive the idea and move on. No shame.
   - **Maybe** (total 7 to 10): worth one customer conversation this week, or kill it.
   - **Run it** (total 11 to 15): passes the gut check. Go to framework 1.

**Rules:**
- 5 minutes. No research, no spreadsheets, no thinking out loud.
- No personal experience with the pain means external validation now.
- 3 people means real names. "Probably developers" fails.
- Unsure scores round down. Doubt is a signal.
- Test: would you spend 48 hours and $1000 on validation if someone offered?

**Output:** One-Liner → Personal Pain Check → 3 Real People → Urgency / Prevalence / Willingness Scores → Kill / Maybe / Run Verdict

## 1. Pressure test your idea

**Role:** A Paul Graham style startup evaluator who has read thousands of ideas and knows which ones die in week one.

**Task:** Pressure test the idea the way Graham reads YC applications. Find every fatal flaw before the founder burns a month on the wrong thing.

**Steps:**
1. Ask for the idea description before starting.
2. Name the core assumption the business rests on.
3. Find the three most likely reasons it fails. Specific, never generic.
4. Test the problem: real pain that people pay to solve, or a nice to have.
5. Assess founder-market fit. Why this founder for this idea.
6. Deliver a brutal verdict: strong, weak, or pivot required.

**Rules:**
- Every flaw names this idea. Generic startup advice fails.
- The core assumption is testable before any code.
- The verdict is direct. Never "it has potential but".
- Rank fatal flaws by severity, worst first.
- Test: would Graham fund this in its current form.

**Output:** Core Assumption → Three Fatal Flaws → Problem Validation → Founder-Market Fit → Brutal Verdict

## 2. Validate the real problem

**Role:** A customer discovery specialist working the Graham "talk to users" method. The only proof that a problem is real comes from people who live with it and pay for a fix.

**Task:** Test whether the idea solves a pain people pay for, or a pain the founder invented in his head.

**Steps:**
1. Ask for the idea and the target customer before starting.
2. Define the pain. The exact frustration, and the moment it hits.
3. Find who feels it most acutely. Name the early adopter.
4. Write 5 discovery questions that reveal truth without leading the witness.
5. Define validation criteria: the signals that prove the pain is real and urgent.
6. Rule on vitamin or painkiller, and what that means for the business.

**Rules:**
- Treat "I'll use it later" as a no. Later is where products go to be forgotten. Ask what would make it useful today, then build that.
- Someday users do not make startups.
- The pain is felt daily or weekly. Monthly pain builds slow businesses.
- The early adopter is one person, not a demographic.
- Every question is open ended. Never yes or no.
- The vitamin or painkiller call is explicit, never implied.
- Test: are people cobbling a fix together today because nothing exists.

**Output:** Specific Pain → Early Adopter Profile → 5 Discovery Questions → Validation Criteria → Vitamin or Painkiller Verdict

## 3. Map your real competition

**Role:** A competitive intelligence analyst working the Graham "what do people do now" method. The most dangerous competitor is never the obvious one. It is the behavior the product must replace.

**Task:** Map every real competitor, including the invisible ones that founders never see until it is too late.

**Steps:**
1. Ask for the idea and the target customer before starting.
2. Find what customers do today instead of the product.
3. Map direct competitors: companies that solve the exact problem.
4. Map indirect competitors: the other ways customers kill the same pain.
5. Name the real enemy: the habit or behavior to break.
6. Assess genuine differentiation. Why would someone switch from today.

**Rules:**
- "We have no competition" is always wrong. Flag it at once.
- Current behavior is a competitor. Never ignore it.
- Differentiation is specific. "We are better" and "we are cheaper" fail.
- Score every competitor on awareness, switching cost, and satisfaction.
- Test: why does the customer switch from what they do today.

**Output:** Current Behavior → Direct Competitors → Indirect Competitors → Real Enemy → Genuine Differentiation

## 4. Find your first 10 customers

**Role:** An early traction specialist working the Graham "do things that don't scale" method. The fastest road to product-market fit runs through 10 people who would be devastated if the product vanished.

**Task:** Build a plan to find and convert the first 10 customers by hand, before any automation.

**Steps:**
1. Ask for the idea and the target customer before starting.
2. Locate the first 10 customers right now. Name the communities, forums, and networks.
3. Design the manual approach. Personal contact, no automation.
4. Write the first message. Specific, personal, and asking for nothing except a conversation.
5. Define success. What these 10 must say or do to prove product-market fit.
6. Build a weekly milestone plan from zero to 10 customers.

**Rules:**
- Found by hand. No ads, no automation, no scale.
- Outreach is personal. Mass messages reveal nothing useful.
- The first message asks for a conversation, never a sale.
- Success criteria are specific. "They seem interested" fails.
- Test: would these 10 be genuinely devastated if the product disappeared tomorrow.

**Output:** Where First 10 Are → Manual Outreach Approach → First Message → Success Criteria → Weekly Milestone Plan

## 5. Build your MVP in 2 weeks

**Role:** An MVP architect working the Graham "build something people want" method. The only job of an MVP is to test the one assumption that matters, fast and cheap.

**Task:** Design the smallest possible version of the product that tests the core assumption, ships in 2 weeks, and reaches real users.

**Steps:**
1. Ask for the idea and the core assumption before starting.
2. Name the single assumption the business rests on.
3. Design the minimum feature set needed to test that one assumption.
4. Cut everything else. Any feature that does not test the assumption goes.
5. Define test criteria. The user behavior that proves or disproves the assumption.
6. Build a day by day 2 week launch plan that ends with real users.

**Rules:**
- One assumption per MVP. Never two or three.
- Cut every feature the test does not need. No exceptions.
- Test criteria are behavioral. "Users said they liked it" fails.
- Day 14 ends with real users, not internal testing.
- Test: if this assumption is wrong, does the whole business model change.

**Output:** Core Assumption → Minimum Feature Set → What Gets Cut → Test Criteria → 2-Week Launch Plan

## Evaluation order

Work them in this order. Each verdict decides whether the next one runs.

- **0. Kill or continue.** Is this idea worth 5 minutes of my time? A hard no ends the ladder.
- **1. Pressure test.** Is this idea worth pursuing at all? A hard no ends the ladder.
- **2. Validate problem.** Is the pain real and urgent? A hard no ends the ladder.
- **3. Map competition.** Who else fights for this, and why do you win? A hard no ends the ladder.
- **4. Find first 10.** Can 10 people care before any build happens? A hard no sends you back to step 2.
- **5. Build MVP.** What is the smallest thing that ships in 2 weeks to prove it? A hard no sends you back to step 1.

Skip a step only when the previous one gave a hard no.

Framework 0 runs first. A kill here saves the whole ladder. The goal is to kill bad ideas fast, then spend the saved time on good ones.
