# Offer testing: the Gauntlet

Read this when the list looks right and replies stay flat, or before you spend money on mapping a market. The method comes from Growth Engine X. Treat its numbers as one agency's campaign data, not as benchmarks.

## Why test the offer first

Assume the list is correct. If the list is correct and nobody answers, two things failed: the offer and the next step. Nothing else.

That is the whole argument for short emails. A five-paragraph message fails for five reasons at once and teaches you nothing. A one-line message fails for one.

## The one-line email

```text
if we could help you {{offer}} without {{risk}}, would that be useful?
```

```text
we just helped {{case study}} {{accomplishment}}. could i show you how we did it?
```

Lowercase, no greeting, no signature, no tracking. The line carries the offer and the ask. If it gets no reply, the offer is the finding.

## Three buckets of friction

Test one bucket at a time, with 3 to 5 angles inside each. The offer changes between buckets. The angle changes inside a bucket.

| Bucket | The ask | Example | What a yes tells you |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | The dream ask. Straight into your sales process. | "do you need help automating your sales tax compliance?" (Numeral) | The market wants the thing at list price. Run it. |
| 2 | The same ask plus free work on your side. | Two meetings booked straight onto their calendar. | The market wants it, but the first ask does not clear the bar. Free work does. |
| 3 | The ask you hope you never need. | Pay for a meeting, a month of free work, buy out their contract. | Where the real friction sits, and what it costs to remove. |

Bucket 2 needs a qualifier, or it attracts the wrong buyer. Put it in the P.S.:

> "P.S. of course these leads are free, to show you we can get meetings. if we're successful, we'll talk about putting you on a flat monthly retainer like the rest of our customers."

That line tells performance-only buyers up front that you do not work that way. Some still reply. Weed them out in the sales process. Treat the P.S. as a variable, because it changes who answers.

## Bucket 3 is a falsification test

You run it to prove a negative, not to win.

Take something from bucket 1 and offer $50 to $200 for a meeting. If cash does not buy a meeting, no copy will. You do not have an offer that converts cold traffic, and you now know that for the price of a few hundred dollars.

If bucket 3 wins, do two things in order. Check it against your cost to acquire a customer. Then go back and rebuild buckets 1 and 2 with what the result taught you about how far the offer has to go.

## How to run it

- 3 buckets, 3 to 5 angles per bucket, one ask per bucket.
- 1,000 sends per sequence before you judge it.
- 7 days between buckets on the same pool, with a new subject line. People do not remember your emails.
- Judge on positive reply rate inside each bucket. Revenue decides the final winner.
- Build the lead magnet after demand shows up, not before. Turn the campaign off and take 24 to 48 hours to build the thing. Nobody wants to discover that the agent they built has no buyers.

## Worked example: commercial waste

Four things buyers valued: price, a consistent driver, an invoice that matches the quote, and compliance with state rules backed by an insurance guarantee.

Three angles, same ask ("could we talk about how our service could fit your needs better?"):

| Angle | Contacted | Reply rate | Interested |
|---|---|---|---|
| Same driver, same day | 6,111 | 5.38% | 2.08% |
| Invoice matches the quote | 10,450 | 2.10% | 1.23% |
| Compliance walkthrough | 3,985 | 3.24% | 1.12% |

The result that surprised them: buyers cared more about retraining a stranger every few weeks than about the bill. The invoice angle felt obvious and lost by more than half.

Then the offer ladder, against a market with 3-year contracts:

- Bucket 1: a meeting to review what working together looks like.
- Bucket 2: pay 3 months of their current vendor contract.
- Bucket 3: buy out the remaining term, up to a full year.

That ladder prices the real objection. In a locked-in market, the reason a buyer says no is rarely the pitch, it is the contract. Buckets 2 and 3 measure how much lock-in you have to remove, and whether the removal still leaves a business.

They never ran buckets 2 and 3, because bucket 1 produced enough leads. That is the correct outcome. Do not buy friction you do not need.

## Reading the result

| Result | What it means | Next move |
|---|---|---|
| Bucket 1 replies | The offer clears cold traffic at full price. | Stop testing, run volume. |
| Bucket 2 wins | Free work clears the bar that the ask alone missed. | Check CAC, then productize the free work. |
| Bucket 3 wins on cash only | No offer clears cold traffic without a subsidy. | Rebuild 1 and 2, or change market. |
| All three flat on a clean list | The market or the list is wrong. | Re-check the list before touching copy again. |

## Honest limits

- The numbers come from agency campaigns in specific industries. They show direction, not a target.
- Positive replies lead meetings. Revenue still decides.
- A one-line email cannot prove a market exists. It proves an offer clears friction.
- One bucket at a time, or you learn nothing from the result.

## Where this sits in the playbook

Run this before layer 3 of the main skill. When contacts per lead stays past 1,500 after angle work, the offer is the problem, and list work will not rescue it.
