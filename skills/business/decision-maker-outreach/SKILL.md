# Decision-Maker Outreach

A system for finding the exact people who can say "yes" and reaching them in a way that feels natural rather than salesy.

The core idea: **you do not find decision-makers by searching harder, you find them by understanding their world well enough that it becomes obvious where they are.** What does their day look like? Where do they spend free time? Which conferences, chats, and communities do they actually value, and which are full of vendors pitching each other? Once you know that, the "where" and the "how" mostly answer themselves.

## B2B vs B2C framing

Figure out which situation the user is in first, because it changes who "the right person" is:

- **B2B.** The decision-maker *is* the customer. Reaching them converts directly into revenue.
- **B2C.** The end user is a consumer, so the decision-makers worth finding sit at **partners**: companies that already own the audience you need. The value exchange can be a technical integration, media or co-marketing, an expertise swap, an introduction, or anything that creates value on both sides. Start by listing which big players already have your audience, what *they* need, and how that turns into money for both of you.

## The workflow

Walk the user through these five stages. If they are already partway in (say, they have a crisp ICP), skip ahead, but check the earlier stage is genuinely solid. Weak ICPs are the most common reason outreach fails.

### 1. Define exactly who you need

"Business founders" or "marketing people" is not an ICP. Push for specifics:

- **Role.** The title of the person who feels the pain *and* controls or strongly influences budget. Note it when these are different people.
- **Company.** Industry, size (users, revenue, headcount), stage, geography.
- **Trigger moment.** The event that makes them need this *now*. This matters most, because timing closes deals. A great product pitched at the wrong moment gets ignored.
- **Pain plus current workaround.** What is broken, and what do they do today instead.
- **Budget reality.** Is there money for this, and whose line item is it?

Help the user write a one-paragraph ICP. Example of the target level of specificity:

> CMO at an iGaming company with roughly 100k to 200k users. They used to buy traffic profitably through Meta, but since Meta rolled out AI optimization their ad accounts get banned too fast and unit economics broke. Our product adds a layer that lets them acquire users at close to their old cost. The CMO owns the acquisition budget and is under pressure to restore growth this quarter.

If the user's ICP is vague, ask pointed questions instead of accepting it. Test: could two different people read the ICP and independently pick out the same 20 companies? If not, it is not specific enough.

### 1a. Define them by the tool they are missing

A founder signed 40 customers in his first quarter with no ads, no content, and no warm intros. He sold an AI product analytics tool by looking at what companies use, then targeting the gap.

Everyone looks at what tools a company has. He looked at what was missing. His ideal customer was not a company using Mixpanel. It was a company that should be using something like Mixpanel but is not yet.

The method:

1. Pick one product category where the absence is obvious.
2. Build lists of companies running the surrounding stack but not this one. For example: Google Analytics plus Hotjar plus Google Tag Manager, and no Segment or Mixpanel.
3. Write the gap in one sentence. A team that watches heat maps and page views but has zero visibility into what users do inside the product has outgrown its stack and has not admitted it yet.
4. Split the list by maturity, because the same gap needs different messaging. WordPress plus GA plus Hotjar is a marketing-led company: "understand which visitors become real users." Shopify plus GA plus Hotjar is an e-commerce brand: "see exactly where buyers drop off between product page and checkout."
5. Reach the head of growth with a mini-audit. Opening line: "Noticed you are using Hotjar and GA but nothing for product events. Curious how you are tracking activation today?"

Most replied with some version of "honestly? Spreadsheets." He got a 31% reply rate and 1 in 5 replies turned into a demo.

Your best prospects may be defined by the tool they lack, not the one they have.

### 2. Customer development (talk to about 10 of them)

Before hunting at scale, get conversations with a handful of people who fit the ICP. **Do not sell.** The goal is to validate assumptions and collect information a search engine cannot give you.

Questions worth asking (adapt to context; `references/playbook-assets.md` has a fuller bank):

- How do you solve this today? What have you already tried?
- Who do you trust for advice on this? Whose opinion changes your mind?
- Where do you get information: newsletters, podcasts, chats, people?
- Which events or communities are actually worth your time, and which are a waste?
- What do you do outside work: sports, clubs, hobbies you share with peers?

After a few conversations, the same names, places, and communities start repeating. **Those repeats are your entry points.** Help the user log answers so the pattern stays visible (a simple table: person / tools tried / trusted sources / valued events / communities / hobbies).

Warn about the conference trap. Events that promise "hundreds of CMOs" are often full of people like the user: vendors trying to sell. The gems tend to be smaller and less advertised. A Saturday padel tournament that senior investment bankers play near Central Park, a private dinner series, an invite-only chat. Customer development is how you hear about these.

To get the first 10 conversations with no network: ask for advice rather than a meeting, lean on second-degree intros, offer something useful in return, and ask every interviewee "who else should I talk to?"

### 3. Pick a natural entry point

For each promising place, online or offline, work out how the user can legitimately be there. Options:

- **Sponsor** or co-host. A small sponsorship of a niche event often buys more access than a booth at a big one.
- **Participate genuinely.** Play in the tournament, join the community as a real member.
- **Go with someone** who already attends, as their guest or teammate.
- **Contribute.** Speak, run a workshop, organize a side event.

The only requirement is that the user's presence feels natural and raises no questions. Keep it honest. Showing up as yourself with a real reason to be there is what makes the later relationship work. Never advise inventing a job title or background to get in. If it comes out, it destroys exactly the trust the whole approach depends on.

### 4. Build the relationship, let them connect the dots

Big B2B deals are a long game built on trust. Coach the user to:

- Make a good impression as a person first. No pitching on first contact.
- Wait for the natural question. Business people eventually ask "so what do you do?"
- Answer briefly, then mention **one recent, concrete case** where they did something impressive for a company just like the listener's.
- Stop there. Let the other person connect the dots and ask the follow-up. A pull feels completely different from a push.

Help the user prepare that one-sentence "what I do" line plus the case story in advance (see templates in the reference file).

### 5. Growth hacks to accelerate

Suggest the ones that fit the user's situation:

- **Find the niche online gathering spots.** Telegram, Slack, and Discord groups, forums, subreddits, private communities. Sometimes hundreds of target people sit in one place. Ask about these during customer development, because members name them. Join as a contributing member and follow each community's rules on promotion.
- **Lower the barrier to talking to you.** Building a relationship is hard when the first interaction is a sales pitch. Give value first: invite them onto a podcast, feature them in a newsletter or report, start a community for people like them, host a dinner. Once the relationship exists, the sale gets much easier. Podcasts and "expert roundups" work especially well, because being asked for an opinion is flattering and low commitment.
- **Dig into niche or dead social networks.** Startups sometimes built a network for one specific audience and attracted a lot of your target people. Active or not, the public profiles there reveal who is in the space. If it is still active, reach out right there.
- **Reach people where they actually respond.** Many decision-makers ignore LinkedIn inboxes and answer on other channels. If someone publicly lists a Telegram, X, or other handle on their profile, site, or talk slides, that is a fair channel. Because the channel is more personal, the bar for the message is higher: short, specific, clearly relevant to them, easy to decline.
- **Write good cold messages.** Whatever the channel, the message decides the outcome. `references/playbook-assets.md` has the structure and examples.

### Three rules that decide most outcomes

1. **Give people a reason to trust you.** When a stranger messages, they check your profile first. It must explain who you are, what you are building, and who it helps. An empty profile kills the message before it is read.
2. **Make the first message easy to answer.** "Would you be open to exploring a partnership?" gets no reply. "Saw you are hiring five engineers. How are you finding candidates?" gets one. Ask about their situation, never about your offer.
3. **Do not automate yet.** The instinct to automate posting and DMs is strong and premature. First learn who cares about the problem and which messages hit the pain point. Automating before you know what works only scales the wrong message.

## Guardrails

This playbook works because it is built on trust, so keep the tactics on the right side of the line:

- **Use contact details people have made available**, not ones pieced together against their wishes. Never recommend tools or workflows that de-anonymize people, scrape private groups, or map an email to hidden accounts to private chats. Beyond the ethics, it is the fastest way to get labeled creepy in a small industry where everyone talks.
- **Respect privacy and anti-spam law.** In the EU and UK (GDPR, ePrivacy), cold B2B email to individuals needs a legitimate-interest basis, relevance to their role, clear sender identity, and an easy opt-out. Many countries restrict unsolicited messages. Raise this when the user plans volume outreach, and suggest they check local rules.
- **Follow platform rules.** Mass DMs, automated scraping, and bots in communities get accounts banned and burn the entry point for good.
- **One follow-up, then stop.** A polite nudge is fine. Repeated chasing on personal channels is not.

If the user asks for something past these lines, say briefly why it backfires and offer the closest effective alternative: warm intros through a mutual, value-first content, or joining the community properly.

## Output format

When the user asks for a plan, produce a **Reach Plan** with these sections. Skip the ones that do not apply, and keep each tight and specific to their case:

1. **ICP.** The one-paragraph profile, plus trigger moment and budget owner.
2. **Hypotheses to validate.** The 3 to 5 assumptions customer development must confirm or kill.
3. **Customer development script.** 8 to 12 questions tailored to their ICP, plus how to land the first 10 calls.
4. **Entry-point candidates.** A table: place / online or offline / why target people are there / how to get in naturally / confidence (guess vs validated).
5. **Relationship plan.** The "what I do" line and the case story to drop in.
6. **Growth hacks.** Only the ones relevant to them, made concrete.
7. **First messages.** 1 to 3 draft outreach messages for their top channels.
8. **Next 2 weeks.** A short, dated action list.

For narrower requests (just a cold message, just an ICP, just a list of communities), answer only that piece. Do not force the full plan.

When the user's industry is specific, use web search (if available) to find real communities, events, newsletters, and podcasts for that audience. Label anything unverified as a hypothesis to confirm in customer development.
